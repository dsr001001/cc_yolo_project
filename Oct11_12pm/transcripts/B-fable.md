## Turn 1 (user)
Hi, I'm in the UK and I've got about £3,000 sitting on a credit card that's charging me 24.9% APR. I want to move it to a 0% balance transfer card so I can actually clear it. Which card should I go for?

## Turn 1 (model)
Good question, and the honest answer is that it depends mostly on how fast you can realistically pay the £3,000 off. Here's where the UK market sits right now (figures from Which?, MSE and Finder, all current as of September/early October 2026, so double-check the live page before applying):

**The two main routes**

1. **Long 0% with a fee (the "safe" option if you need 2 to 3 years)**
   - **HSBC Balance Transfer card**: 36 months at 0%, ~3.1% fee (about £93 on £3,000), sometimes with £25 cashback that nearly halves that fee. MSE's current top pick.
   - **Virgin Money 36 Month Balance Transfer**: same deal, 36 months, 3.1% fee.
   - **NatWest 36-month card**: 36 months, 3.1% fee.
   - **TSB Platinum**: the longest at 38 months, but a higher 3.49% fee (~£105). Only worth the extra over HSBC/Virgin if you genuinely need those two extra months.

2. **Shorter 0% with no fee (cheaper if you can clear it in about a year)**
   - **RBS Balance Transfer**: 13 months at 0%, no fee (transfer within 3 months of opening).
   - **Santander Everyday No Balance Transfer Fee**: 12 months at 0%, no fee.
   - **Barclaycard** also has a 12-month no-fee offer.

**Quick maths on £3,000**
- Clear it in 12 months: about £250/month, costs you £0 on a no-fee card.
- Clear it in 36 months: about £83/month, costs you the ~£93 fee (or ~£68 with HSBC's cashback). Compare that to roughly £700+ in interest if it stayed at 24.9%.

**My recommendation:** if £250/month is a stretch, go for the **HSBC 36-month card** (lowest fee at the long end, with the cashback sweetener). If you can comfortably throw £250+ a month at it, the **RBS 13-month no-fee card** is cheaper. A common middle path is a 36-month card for safety and overpaying anyway; the fee is only ~£90 of insurance.

Before you apply, a few things that matter more than the card choice:
- Use an **eligibility checker** (MSE's or Which?'s) first; it's a soft search and tells you which cards you're likely to get without denting your credit file. The 36-month cards need a good credit score, and you might be offered a shorter period than advertised.
- Transfer the balance **within the window** (usually 60 to 90 days), always pay at least the minimum, and **don't spend on the new card** (purchases usually aren't at 0%).
- Set a direct debit for roughly balance divided by months so it's cleared before the 0% ends, then close or stop using the old card.

Thanks, that's really helpful detail, and it changes the answer. Two things first:

- **Virgin Money is out for you.** Its cards moved under Nationwide in April 2026, and Nationwide's balance transfer card (30 months, 2.99% fee) now requires a Nationwide current account, savings account or mortgage, which you don't have.
- **The HSBC/NatWest/TSB 36 to 38 month cards are aimed at "good to excellent" credit.** With a Fair band and a missed payment inside the last two years, a cold application to those is a coin flip at best, and a rejection leaves a hard search on your file right before you try the next one. So I wouldn't lead with them.

The good news is that a single missed payment 20 months ago is pretty mild: lenders weight the last 12 months most heavily, you're on the electoral roll, and everything else is clean. That puts you squarely in the group Barclaycard is set up to serve.

**My one pick: Barclaycard Platinum Balance Transfer card**

Why this one:
- Barclaycard runs a **tiered "up to" offer** rather than a hard pass/fail. Its headline is up to 36 months at around 3.15% fee, but applicants with Fair credit are typically offered a shorter term (often somewhere in the 20 to 30 month range) instead of being declined outright. Even the lower end of that covers your 18 to 24 month plan with a bit of buffer.
- It doesn't rely on an existing banking relationship, unlike Nationwide, and it's historically more accepting of near-prime applicants than HSBC or NatWest.
- The fee is a hair over your 3% ceiling (3.15% is about £94.50 on £3,000, so roughly £4.50 more than a 3% card). Not worth sacrificing acceptance odds over.
- Credit limits are usually sensible enough to take the full £3,000, which matters because the Fair-credit specialist lenders often start you at £1,000 to £1,500.

**Backup if Barclaycard shows a low chance: Vanquis Low APR Balance Transfer card.** 36 months at 0% with no transfer fee at all, and Vanquis is a near-prime lender that actively accepts Fair-band applicants. The catches are a 35.9% APR afterwards (irrelevant if you clear it in time) and the risk of a starting limit below £3,000, which would mean only part of the debt moves.

**How to do it so you only apply once:**
1. Run the **MSE Credit Club eligibility checker** (soft search, no footprint). It gives you a percentage chance per card and, crucially, will usually say what 0% length Barclaycard would actually give you, not the headline. If Barclaycard comes back 70%+ or "pre-approved", apply for that. If it's weak and Vanquis is strong, go Vanquis.
2. Apply for one card only. Wait at least a few weeks before trying another if declined.
3. Once approved, transfer within the window (Barclaycard's is 60 days), set a direct debit for about £150 a month minimum, don't spend on the new card, and stop using the old one.

At £150 to £200 a month you'll be clear in 15 to 20 months, so even a 24-month offer gives you real breathing room, and the fee works out to a few pounds a month versus the roughly £60 a month in interest you're paying now.

Thanks. If I get declined for that one, what's my next best option? And is there anything that could trip me up along the way?

## Turn 3 (model)
Good questions, and worth having the fallback mapped out before you apply rather than after.

**If Barclaycard declines you**

1. **Vanquis Low APR Balance Transfer** is the clear next step: 36 months at 0%, no transfer fee, and Vanquis is built for exactly your credit profile. It's genuinely the cheapest deal in the whole market if you're accepted and clear it in time. The things to watch: the 35.9% APR afterwards (so you must be done inside the 36 months), and the starting limit, which may be under £3,000. If they offer you, say, £2,000, take it, move £2,000 across and attack the remaining £1,000 on the old card first since it's still at 24.9%.
2. **Fair-credit cards with shorter 0% periods** (Fluid, Aqua, Capital One, Tesco's "fair credit" tier): usually 9 to 20 months at 0% with a fee around 3 to 4%. These sit below Vanquis for you because the periods are tight for a 2-year plan and the limits are often small.
3. **If everything declines:** stop applying. Spend 3 to 6 months paying down what you can on the current card, wait for the missed payment to pass the 2-year mark, and re-run the eligibility checker. One rejection isn't a disaster; three in a row with hard searches is what actually damages your score.

Timing tip: if Barclaycard declines, run the eligibility checker again before applying to Vanquis rather than applying straight away. Leave a couple of weeks between hard searches if you can.

**Things that could trip you up**
- **The "up to" trap.** If Barclaycard offers you, say, 22 months rather than 36, that's the deal, and it's still fine for your plan. Just make sure you read the actual offer letter and base your repayment plan on the real end date, not the advert.
- **Missing the transfer window.** Barclaycard gives 60 days from account opening to request the transfer; some cards give 90. Request it the day the card arrives. If you miss the window, you've got a card with no 0% on it.
- **A limit lower than £3,000.** Lenders often cap transfers at around 90 to 95% of your limit, so a £3,200 limit might only let you move £2,900. Move as much as you can and prioritise the leftover on the old card.
- **One late payment kills the 0%.** Most issuers reserve the right to withdraw the promotional rate if you miss a payment, and it would also add a fresh missed payment to your file. Set a direct debit for a fixed amount (£150+) the day the account opens, not just the minimum.
- **Spending on the new card.** Purchases typically aren't at 0% on a balance transfer card, and if they are it's usually for a short window. Keep the new card in a drawer.
- **The fee goes on the balance.** The ~£95 fee is added to the transferred amount, so you'll owe about £3,095 at 0%. Budget for it so you're not caught out at the end.
- **The old card's final statement.** Interest accrues daily, so a few pounds of residual interest usually appears after the transfer goes through. Check the old account a month later and clear any small remainder, otherwise it sits there racking up 24.9% and possibly a late fee.
- **Don't rush to close the old card.** Once it's at zero, leaving it open (unused) actually helps your credit utilisation while you rebuild. Close it later if you don't trust yourself with it, or ask the lender to lower the limit.
- **Changing your details right before applying.** Lenders like stability. Don't change address, job or bank account in the weeks around the application if you can avoid it, and make sure the address you give exactly matches the electoral roll entry.
- **The end of the 0% period.** Put a reminder in your calendar two months before it ends. If there's any balance left at that point, you can shift it again, but only if your credit file is in good shape, which brings it back to paying on time every month.

You're in a better position than you might think: with £150 to £200 a month you'll likely be clear with months to spare, and 18 to 24 months of clean payments on a mainstream card will push you out of the Fair band too.
